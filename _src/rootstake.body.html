The module

A little house for the brain.

Together with the Rooted SG app, this module is Meco (heymeco.xyz). The module is a small house that stands on its own: a gable roof carries two 70 mm solar panels over an ESP32-S3. There is no arm and no gooseneck. The soil probe and the stem electrode are separate parts that connect back to the house by a thin wire, and by the watering tube for the probe. No screws anywhere: parts press-fit, twist-lock or hot-glue.

Power and radio

A battery, stretched by the sun. A radio that mostly sleeps.

01 — Battery + solar

The module runs on a battery, but two 70 mm solar panels on the roof keep topping it up through the day, so the battery drains far more slowly than it would alone. With the panels helping, the module can run for up to two months before the battery needs any attention.

Battery2 × 70 mm solarUp to two months
02 — Low-energy radio, still quick

Everything runs on Bluetooth Low Energy. The module sleeps almost all of the time, wakes, reads its sensors, packs one 46-byte frame and sends it in a quick burst, then goes straight back to sleep. A tiny frame and a short radio-on window keep the energy use very low without making the update slow: the app still hears from the stake quickly.

Prototype — 01 · What it is

Two micro-needles in the stem.

The stem electrode is a small sprung clip carrying two extremely thin titanium micro-needles. When the clip closes, the needles pass through the outer skin of the stem and into the wet, conducting tissue just under it. A thin wire runs from the clip back to the house module. The plant isn't cut or harmed: it is the size of a pinprick.

02 · What it does

It asks the stem how easily current flows.

The module pushes a tiny alternating current through one needle and listens at the other. How much voltage it takes to push that current through is the stem's electrical impedance. Water and dissolved salts carry current; cell membranes get in the way. So the number is really a measure of how wet, how full and how intact the tissue is.

1 kHz · low frequency

Slow current goes around the cells.

At a low frequency the current can't get through a living cell membrane, so it threads the wet gaps between cells. This reading mostly tells us about the water between the cells. Watch the arc bow out around them.

100 kHz · high frequency

Fast current goes straight through.

At a high frequency the current slips through the membranes and passes through the inside of the cells too. This reading tells us about the cells themselves. The path straightens out.

03 · The ratio

The gap between the two is the measurement.

In healthy, full, intact tissue the two readings are far apart, because the membranes block the slow current and not the fast one. If cells dry out or are damaged, membranes leak and the gap closes. The module sweeps from 1 kHz up to 100 kHz and watches how that gap changes over hours and days.

04 · What it's for

An early word from the plant, before it wilts.

The stem can change before a leaf droops. So the electrode is an early, from-the-inside signal of water stress or tissue damage, checked against the soil and air readings before the app says anything. It is a prototype: we report trend and timing, never a calibrated figure.

02 — The plant

The house, the probe and the clip belong to one plant.

The house module stands beside the plant. The soil probe is in the soil, joined by wire and the watering tube. The stem electrode is on the stem, joined by its own thin wire. Everything the module knows comes from this one plant.

03 — The stem

Two micro-needles go in.

The clip closes and the two extremely thin titanium needles slip through the outer skin of the stem, about a pinprick each, and stop in the conducting tissue.

04 — Inside the stem

Skin, cortex, and a ring of pipes.

Cut across, a stem has an outer skin (epidermis), a cortex of soft cells, a ring of vascular bundles, and a pith in the middle. In each bundle the xylem carries water up and the phloem carries sugars. The needle tips sit in that ring.

05 — The cells

Down to single cells.

Each cell has a wall, a thin membrane inside it, a big water-filled vacuole, a nucleus and chloroplasts. The xylem vessels are wide, hollow pipes; the phloem sieve tubes have companion cells that look after them. The membranes are what the current has to deal with.

06 — The current

Slow goes around. Fast goes through.

A tiny current runs between the two needles. At 1 kHz it can't cross membranes, so it winds around the cells. At 100 kHz it passes straight through them. Healthy tissue shows a big difference between the two.

07 — The fungus

A fungal colony moves in.

Some fungal diseases colonise the xylem. Thread-like hyphae grow up through the vessels, branch, and push through the walls into neighbouring cells, until a colony is living inside the plant. This happens inside the stem, long before it shows on a leaf.

08 — The damage

The tissue stops behaving like tissue.

Hyphae and spores clog the vessels. Membranes of nearby cells start to leak. The wall between "around" and "through" gets thinner, and that is exactly what the current feels.

09 — The signal

The two readings move together.

Leaky membranes no longer block the slow current, so the gap between the 1 kHz and 100 kHz readings closes. The module spots that shift in the trend over time. The numbers on this drawing are illustrative, not measurements.

10 — The alert

This plant is flagged. Only this plant.

The app says the stem reading on this plant has changed and asks you to check it. One module reads its own plant: it can't see the neighbours' tissue and it can't name the fungus. It is a prototype early warning, to be confirmed by looking at the plant.
